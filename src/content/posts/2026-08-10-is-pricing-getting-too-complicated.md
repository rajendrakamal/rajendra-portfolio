Open the pricing page of almost any modern SaaS product and you will find some combination of seats, usage tiers, credits, overage charges, and feature gates stacked on top of each other. A decade ago, most software was priced with a single lever: pick a plan, pay a monthly fee, done. Today it is common to see three or four pricing dimensions applied at once. The question worth asking is whether that added sophistication is actually serving customers, or just serving the vendor's revenue model.

## Why pricing got more complicated in the first place

The honest answer is that complexity usually starts as a genuine improvement. Flat pricing forces every customer into the same bucket regardless of how much value they actually extract from a product, and that mismatch creates real problems. A customer processing ten records a month and a customer processing ten million should not pay the same amount, and usage-based pricing fixes that. Tiered plans let a vendor serve a price-sensitive small business and a demanding enterprise account without either one subsidizing the other. Add-ons let customers pay only for the capability they need instead of a bloated all-in-one package.

Each of these mechanisms, on its own, is a reasonable response to a real problem: value delivered is not uniform across customers, so price should not be either.

## Where it starts to break down

The trouble is that these mechanisms rarely arrive one at a time. A product starts with seat-based pricing, then adds a usage cap because seats alone undercharge heavy users, then adds a premium tier because usage caps alone undersell advanced features, then adds metered overages because the tier boundaries created a cliff customers were gaming. Each individual decision made sense in a planning meeting. The cumulative result is a bill that a customer cannot predict without opening a calculator.

That unpredictability has a cost that rarely shows up in the pricing model itself: it shows up in support tickets, in finance teams pushing back at renewal time, and in the kind of quiet churn where a customer leaves not because a competitor is better, but because they were tired of being surprised by their own invoice.

## A simple test for whether complexity is earned

The heuristic I keep coming back to is this: a customer should be able to explain, in one sentence, why their bill is what it is. "We pay for twenty seats" passes that test. "We pay a base fee plus usage credits that roll over quarterly, minus a volume discount that kicks in above a threshold that resets annually" does not, even if every individual piece of that structure was designed with good intentions.

If a customer cannot reconstruct their own bill from memory, the pricing model has usually outrun the value story that is supposed to justify it. That is a signal worth taking seriously, because pricing is one of the few parts of a product that every customer is forced to understand, whether or not they ever open the app.

## A few practical guardrails

A handful of habits tend to keep pricing complexity in check without giving up its benefits.

Limit the number of independent levers. Seats, usage, and feature tier can each be a reasonable axis, but stacking three or four of them at once multiplies the number of edge cases a customer has to reason about, and multiplies the number of ways the model can produce a bill nobody predicted.

Anchor to a metric the customer already tracks. Usage-based pricing works best when it is billed against something the customer independently measures and cares about, such as active users or transactions processed. It works poorly when it is billed against an internal system metric the customer has never seen and has no way to monitor themselves.

Make the ceiling visible before the floor. Customers forgive a higher starting price far more easily than they forgive an unbounded one. A plan with a known maximum, even a generous one, creates far less anxiety than a plan where the bill is technically unlimited.

## The takeaway

Pricing complexity is not inherently a mistake. Segmenting by value, aligning cost to usage, and letting customers pay only for what they need are all legitimate reasons to move past a single flat fee. But complexity has to earn its keep, and the simplest test of that is whether a customer can explain their own bill without help. When they cannot, the pricing model has stopped serving the business it was built to protect.
